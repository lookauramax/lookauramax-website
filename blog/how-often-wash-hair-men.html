How Often Should Men Wash Their Hair?

By Lookauramax Team · Last updated: 23 Oct 2026 · General information, not medical advice.

For most men, the honest answer is two to three times a week. That is the dermatologist range for an average scalp. But your number moves: fine or oily hair often needs washing every other day, sometimes daily, while dry, thick, or very curly hair can go a week or more between washes and be better for it. This guide helps you find your number, shows what happens at both extremes, and covers how to wash properly.

There is no single right number. There is a right number for your scalp, and it is not hard to find.

The short answer, by scalp and hair type

Start here. Find the row that sounds like your head, and treat it as your starting point.

  • Oily scalp, or fine hair that goes flat and greasy fast: every other day — and daily is fine if the oil genuinely bothers you. Cleveland Clinic dermatologist Dr Shilpi Khetarpal notes that people with oily, greasy scalps may want to wash on a daily basis.
  • Average scalp, straight or wavy hair: two to three times a week. This is the range that keeps oil and the scalp's normal yeast in balance for most men.
  • Thick, dry, or wavy-to-curly hair: once or twice a week. Your hair shows oil slowly and loses moisture fast, so frequent washing costs more than it buys.
  • Coily or tightly curled hair: the longest gaps are normal here. At least every two weeks is the floor dermatologists give, and many men do well washing weekly; dry hair types benefit from fewer washes.
  • Short hair usually needs more washing than long hair. Barbers tend to suggest every other day for short cuts and every three to four days for longer hair — oil reaches short ends quickly, and there is less hair to absorb it.

Why your scalp sets the schedule

Your scalp is skin, and like the rest of your skin it produces oil — sebum — around the clock. Shampoo has one job: take the oil, sweat, and yesterday's product off your scalp. That is all it needs to do. Wash frequency is really about how fast your scalp refills that oil, and what happens while it sits there.

Wash too rarely and the oil does not just sit. The normal yeast that lives on every scalp feeds on it, and when that yeast overgrows you get the familiar trio: itch, flakes, and dandruff. Bacteria join in, and hair starts to smell like scalp. Dermatologists put it plainly — under-washing leads to overgrowth of bacteria and yeast, and the itch and flakes follow.

Wash too often and you strip the oil your hair was using. The result is dry, frizzy hair that snaps — washed-out in the literal sense. Longer and curlier hair suffers first, because oil travels slowly down a curl and the ends are old, dry, and fragile.

Neither mistake shows inside a single week. Both compound over months — which is why the schedule matters more than any product on your shelf.

The things that move your number

Texture and thickness. Fine hair shows oil within a day because there is little hair to hold it. Coarse and coily hair slows oil's journey down the strand, which is why it tolerates — and often prefers — long gaps.

Age. Scalp oil production slows as you get older, so the right number at 45 is usually lower than it was at 22. If your routine has not changed in a decade, it may be due a review.

Product. Wax, clay, and pomade do not rinse out with water alone. Heavy product users need shampoo on heavy product days, before build-up turns into itch.

Climate. Hot, humid weather loads the scalp with sweat and speeds oil up. The same man can honestly need an extra wash a week in summer that he does not need in winter.

Sweat, workouts, and the rinse-only day

Daily training does not force daily shampooing. Sweat is mostly water and salt; a thorough water-only rinse after a workout shifts it without stripping the scalp. Save shampoo for your scheduled days and rinse in between. If your scalp still feels loaded after rinsing — heavy product, helmet hours, a genuinely oily scalp — that is a shampoo day regardless of the plan. The schedule serves the scalp, not the other way round.

If you have dandruff, the answer changes

Dandruff flips the usual advice. Because flakes feed on oil and yeast build-up, the fix starts with washing more regularly, not less: two to three times a week with an anti-dandruff shampoo, worked into the scalp and left there for a few minutes before rinsing. Give it a few weeks. If flakes, redness, or itching persist — or the scalp is sore, cracked, or weeping — stop experimenting and see a dermatologist. That is a skin condition asking for a diagnosis, not a stronger shampoo.

How to wash so each wash counts

  1. Soak your hair fully with lukewarm water. Hot water feels good and dries the scalp out; lukewarm does the cleaning without the cost.
  2. Use a coin-sized amount of shampoo. Work the shampoo into your scalp with your fingertips for a slow count of sixty — the scalp is what you are washing; the lengths get cleaned by the rinse running through them.
  3. Rinse longer than feels necessary. Leftover shampoo film is a common, invisible cause of itch that men blame on dandruff.
  4. Condition the lengths and ends, not the scalp. Leave it a minute or two, then rinse. Short hair may skip this on some washes; longer, dry, or curly hair should not.
  5. Pat dry with the towel instead of scrubbing. Wet hair is weak hair, and a rough towel rub is quiet breakage.
  6. Wash your comb or brush once a week while you are at it — otherwise it puts yesterday's oil and product straight back.

None of this needs a shelf of products. Technique beats volume; our hair grooming guide covers the wider routine, and the haircut guide the cut itself.

Two myths worth dropping

Myth one: washing your hair makes it fall out. The hairs in the drain were already shed — everyone loses dozens of hairs a day as part of the normal growth cycle, and washing simply collects the ones that were already loose. Shampoo does not reach the follicle's growth switch. If you are thinning at the crown or temples, that is genetics and hormones, and a dermatologist is the honest next step — not a gentler wash schedule.

Myth two: you can train your scalp to produce less oil by washing less. Oil production is set by hormones and genetics, not by habit; your scalp does not learn. What changes when men stretch their washes is tolerance — they notice the oil less, and their styling adapts. If stretching works for you, it is because your scalp was never that oily, not because it was retrained.

Honest limits: the evidence is not one-way

Here is the part most guides skip: the research does not all point the same way. Dermatology guidance emphasises hair type, as this guide does — but at least one scalp-health study found that washing five to six times a week produced the best scalp condition in its participants. Both can be true for different heads. Frequency advice is a starting range, not a law.

So run the test. Start at three washes a week. Then let your scalp vote: itchy and flaky means wash more; tight and dry means wash less. Two weeks at one setting tells you more than any article — including this one. Adjust one step at a time, and keep the setting that leaves your scalp calm the day after a wash, not just the hour after.

Frequently asked questions

Is it OK for a man to wash his hair every day?

For some men, yes. If your scalp is oily or your hair is fine, daily washing with a gentle shampoo is fine — dermatologists say an oily scalp can be washed daily if it bothers you. If your hair is dry, thick, curly, or long, daily washing strips more oil than your hair can spare, and every other day or less suits it better. The honest test is your scalp: greasy and itchy means wash more; tight, dry, and flaky means wash less or switch to a gentler shampoo.

Should men use conditioner, or is shampoo enough?

For most men, use both — they do different jobs. Shampoo cleans the scalp; conditioner replaces moisture and smoothness on the hair itself, especially the ends. Put conditioner on the lengths and ends, not the scalp, leave it a minute or two, and rinse. Men with very short hair may not feel the difference; anyone with longer, dry, or curly hair will.

How often should I wash my hair if I have dandruff?

More regularly, not less. Dandruff thrives when oil and scalp yeast build up, so washing two to three times a week with an anti-dandruff shampoo is the usual starting point — lather, leave it on the scalp for a few minutes, then rinse. If flakes, itching, or redness persist after a few weeks, or the skin is sore or cracked, see a dermatologist rather than washing harder.

Can I just rinse with water between washes?

Yes — a water-only rinse is a good in-between move. It shifts sweat, dust, and some surface oil without stripping the scalp, which is why many men rinse after a workout and save shampoo for their scheduled wash days. It will not remove heavy styling product or a very oily scalp on its own, but as a bridge it works well.
