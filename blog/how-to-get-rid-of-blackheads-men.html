How to Get Rid of Blackheads?

By Lookauramax Team · Last updated: 10 Nov 2026 · General information, not medical advice.

A blackhead is a pore clogged with oil and dead skin, left open to the air. The dark tip is the plug darkening where it meets the air — not dirt. So scrubbing will not clear it. What clears it is slower and duller: a retinoid at night and a salicylic acid wash, used every day for six to eight weeks. That is what dermatologists recommend.

What a blackhead actually is

A blackhead is a mild form of acne. The medical name is an open comedo. Every hair on your face grows out of a pore, and each pore has a small oil gland attached to it. When dead skin cells do not shed the way they should, they stick together inside the pore with the oil, and a plug forms. Because the pore stays open, the top of the plug meets the air and darkens. That darkening is oxidation. It is the same change that turns a cut apple brown. It is not dirt, and it is not a sign that you wash your face too little.

That one fact explains most of the mistakes men make with blackheads. If the dark colour were dirt, a hard scrub would fix it. It is not dirt, so scrubbing does nothing to the plug and leaves the skin around it raw. The plug sits below the surface a washcloth can reach. Anything that works has to work inside the pore.

Blackheads are not contagious, and they are harmless in themselves. They come back when the routine that kept them away stops. The fix is a routine, not a rescue.

Why they form — and why the nose leads

Four things line up for a blackhead to form, and the Cleveland Clinic lists them plainly: more oil than the pore can clear, dead skin cells that stick together instead of shedding, a rise in androgen hormones, and bacteria on the skin. Any one of them alone rarely does it. Together, they clog the pore.

Oil is the main driver, which is why blackheads cluster where the oil glands are busiest: the nose first, then the forehead and chin — the T-zone. The nose has the densest oil glands on the face, and its pores are wider, so plugs show up there first and stay visible longest. A dotted nose and clear cheeks is normal — it means the glands are doing their job, not that the nose is dirty.

Hormones set the oil level. Androgen hormones rise in the teens and stay part of the picture in adult men, and stress and poor sleep can both push oil production up for a while. That is also why blackheads often get worse in summer.

Dead skin is the quieter half of the problem. A healthy pore clears its own dead cells, but when the cells lining the pore stick together instead of shedding, they form the walls of the plug. That is why the treatments that work change how skin sheds, not the oil on the surface.

What does not cause blackheads: poor hygiene. Over-washing can irritate the skin and make it look worse, because irritated skin sheds badly and produces more oil to replace what was stripped away. If you wash your face three times a day and the blackheads are still there, the washing was never the answer.

The two ingredients that do the work

Dermatology guidance is unusually clear on this one. The American Academy of Dermatology says: "To unclog pores, dermatologists recommend using a retinoid. You can buy one retinoid, adapalene, without a prescription. You'll also want to use a benzoyl peroxide wash." The AAD's 2024 acne guideline gives its strongest recommendations to topical retinoids and benzoyl peroxide, and a weaker, conditional recommendation to salicylic acid. Those three ingredients are the whole treatment.

A retinoid, at night. A retinoid is a medicine made from vitamin A. It speeds up cell turnover and stops skin cells sticking together inside the pore, so new plugs stop forming and old ones slowly clear. Adapalene 0.1% gel is the one you can buy without a prescription in many countries. Use a pea-sized amount, spread thinly over the whole blackhead-prone area, not dabbed onto each dot — the plugs you cannot see yet are the ones it prevents. Start every other night for the first two weeks, because retinoids can dry the skin and make it peel at first. That early peeling is normal, and it settles.

Salicylic acid, in the morning. Salicylic acid is a BHA, and it is the one acid that dissolves in oil, so it can work inside the pore instead of only on the surface. The AAD puts it plainly: "Because it unclogs pores and exfoliates the skin, salicylic acid works best on whiteheads and blackheads." Cleveland Clinic dermatologist Dr. Angela Wei calls a salicylic acid wash her go-to for blackheads, starting at 2% to 4%. A wash is the easiest form — it works in the minute it sits on your face, then rinses off. A leave-on gel is stronger, but it dries the skin out more. Start with the wash.

A benzoyl peroxide wash, as the supporting act. Benzoyl peroxide kills the bacteria that cause acne, but it does not directly clear blackheads — blackheads are not inflamed, so there is no bacteria problem to kill inside them. Its job is to keep the pimples that often come with blackheads under control. If your blackheads come alone, without red pimples, you can skip it. If they come with breakouts, a benzoyl peroxide wash in the morning with the retinoid at night is the combination the AAD recommends — our acne and pimples guide covers it in more detail.

Azelaic acid, for sensitive skin. If a retinoid or salicylic acid leaves your skin red and stinging after two weeks, azelaic acid is the gentler option — less potent on blackheads, but kinder to sensitive skin.

One honest note: the evidence is strongest for acne broadly, not for blackheads alone. The single trial behind salicylic acid found an 11% greater reduction in open comedones than a dummy product after twelve weeks — real, but modest. Retinoids have the stronger record. Neither works in days. The AAD's own rule is six to eight weeks before you judge a product, and three to four months for full clearing.

A routine that fits in five minutes

The routine is short on purpose. Two products that do the work, a moisturiser to keep the skin calm, and sunscreen in the morning. Add one active at a time, a fortnight apart, so you know which one your skin objects to.

Morning. Wash with lukewarm water and a gentle cleanser — or a salicylic acid wash, two or three mornings a week to start, building to daily. Pat dry, never rub. Moisturise with a light moisturiser that says non-comedogenic on the label — made not to clog pores. Finish with sunscreen: retinoids make skin more sensitive to sun, so it stops being optional the week you start one. Our face wash guide and clear skin guide cover the washing and moisturising steps.

Night. Wash again, gently, and wait until the skin is fully dry — a retinoid on damp skin stings more and sinks in unevenly. Spread a pea-sized amount of adapalene over the nose, forehead, and chin. Moisturise over it once it has dried. That is the whole evening routine. On nights your skin feels tight or flaky, skip the retinoid and moisturise only.

The first month. Expect little visible change for the first three weeks, and some dryness and flaking in the first two. The AAD warns that acne can look worse in the early weeks of retinoid use before it improves — that is normal, and it is the point where most men quit. Judge the routine at six to eight weeks, not at two. Take a photo of your nose in the same light on day one and again at week six. The mirror lies about slow change. A photo does not.

What does not work

Squeezing. The AAD advises against it, and the reason is mechanical: pressing on a pore pushes the plug deeper as often as it pushes it out. The pressure can burst the pore wall under the skin and turn a blackhead into an inflamed pimple, and repeated squeezing can leave a mark that outlasts the blackhead by months. If a blackhead needs emptying, a dermatologist does it with sterile tools — the safe route, and the only one that does not risk a scar.

Pore strips. A strip pulls off the dark tip, so the pore looks clear for a day. The clog underneath stays, and the blackhead comes back. Strips can also pull off the top layer of skin and irritate the pore. They are not a treatment. They only work for a photo.

Harsh scrubs and brushes. Scrubbing cannot reach inside the pore, and it can scratch the skin around it. Irritated skin sheds badly, which is one of the things that forms plugs. Our exfoliation guide covers how often to exfoliate without doing damage.

Baking soda, toothpaste, lemon, and kitchen mixtures. These are alkaline or acidic enough to strip the skin's surface, and none of them contains anything that works inside a pore. They irritate first and help never. Keep them in the kitchen.

Pore vacuums. Suction can pull out the loose top of a plug and leave broken blood vessels behind. Dermatologists warn against them for the same reason they warn against squeezing: force applied to a pore usually does more harm than the plug did.

Drying the skin out. Strong astringents and alcohol-heavy toners make the skin feel tight and clean for an hour. Then the oil glands answer by producing more oil, because the skin reads dryness as damage.

The lookalikes on your nose: sebaceous filaments

Many of the dots men call blackheads on the nose are not blackheads at all. They are sebaceous filaments — tiny grey or yellowish dots that sit evenly across the nose, one in every pore, in a neat pattern. A blackhead is darker, larger, and sits in one pore while its neighbours stay clear. A filament is part of how the pore works: it is the channel that carries oil to the surface.

The difference matters because no product removes filaments. Squeeze one out and it refills within about a month. A salicylic acid wash or a retinoid can make filaments less obvious, but they do not go away, and chasing them with strips or squeezing only irritates the nose. If every pore on your nose shows the same small grey dot, those are filaments — leave them alone and let them fade into the background.

When to see a dermatologist

Most blackheads are a routine problem, not a doctor problem. See a dermatologist when the picture changes: deep, painful pimples; scarring; breakouts spreading to the chest or back; or nothing changed after eight to twelve weeks on a retinoid and salicylic acid. A dermatologist can prescribe a stronger retinoid such as tretinoin, or do a professional extraction — sterile tools, a trained hand, no squeezing at home.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get rid of blackheads on my nose?

The nose is the oiliest part of the face, so it gets the most blackheads. The same routine applies: a salicylic acid wash and a nightly retinoid over the whole T-zone, given six to eight weeks. Pore strips only lift the dark tip for a day, and the plug stays.

Do pore strips work on blackheads?

Only on the surface. A strip pulls off the dark tip, so the pore looks clear for a day, but the clog underneath stays and the blackhead comes back. They can also irritate the skin. They are not a treatment.

Can I squeeze blackheads?

Dermatologists advise against it. Squeezing can push the plug deeper, inflame the pore, and leave a mark or a scar. If a blackhead needs emptying, a dermatologist does it with sterile tools — that is the safe route.

How do I stop blackheads coming back?

Keep the routine going after the blackheads clear: a gentle wash, salicylic acid or a retinoid a few nights a week, products that say non-comedogenic, and sunscreen. Blackheads return without maintenance — the routine is the prevention.

Disclaimer: This post is general information, not medical advice. If a blackhead becomes painful, swollen, or leaves a scar, or your skin stays red and irritated after treatment, see a doctor or dermatologist — those need an in-person look, not a guide.
