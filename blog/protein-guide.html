Protein: How Much You Actually Need

By Lookauramax Team · Published 17 Oct 2026 · General information, not medical advice.

Protein is the most over-hyped and under-eaten nutrient at the same time. Here is how much you actually need, where to get it, and which claims to ignore.

Protein builds and repairs muscle, skin, hair, and nails, and it keeps you full. Most people who train do not eat quite enough of it, while supplement marketing makes it sound like you need 300 grams a day. The truth sits in the middle — and it is easy to reach with ordinary food.

The numbers, plainly

  • Sedentary adults: roughly 0.8–1.0 grams per kilogram of body weight per day.
  • Active people / training regularly: roughly 1.4–2.0 grams per kilogram per day.
  • Gaining muscle or dieting while training: the upper end of that range, around 1.6–2.0 grams per kilogram.

Example: a 70 kg person who trains regularly needs roughly 100–140 grams of protein per day. That sounds like a lot until you spread it across meals: 25–35 grams at each of four meals. More than 2.0 g/kg rarely helps for most people — the extra protein mostly becomes expensive calories.

If you have kidney disease, liver disease, or another medical condition, follow your doctor's guidance instead of general ranges.

Spread it across the day

Your body uses protein best when it arrives regularly. Three or four protein-containing meals beats one giant protein dinner. A simple pattern: protein at breakfast (eggs, curd, milk), at lunch (dal, paneer, chicken, or soy), at a snack (nuts, roasted chana, yoghurt), and at dinner (lentils, fish, eggs, or paneer). Each meal does not need to be perfect — the daily total matters most.

Best food sources

  • Animal: eggs, milk, curd, paneer, chicken, fish, lean meat.
  • Plant: lentils, chickpeas, kidney beans, soy and tofu, quinoa, nuts, seeds.
  • Combos that work: rice + dal, roti + paneer, oats + milk + nuts, curd + fruit + seeds.

Plant proteins are slightly less complete on their own, but variety across the day solves that: different plant foods cover each other's gaps. You do not need to combine proteins in a single meal — a varied day is enough.

Vegetarian? You can get there

Yes — vegetarian diets can easily meet protein needs with planning. Anchor each meal with one protein source: paneer, curd, dal, soy, or eggs if you eat them. A sample day: breakfast of oats with milk and nuts; lunch of rice, dal, and curd; snack of roasted chana; dinner of roti, paneer, and vegetables. That day lands near 90–110 grams without any supplement. If you eat very little dairy and no eggs or soy, planning matters more — consider a dietitian's help.

Protein powder: optional, not required

Protein powder is a convenience food, not a magic ingredient. It helps when you are busy, travelling, or struggling to eat enough from meals. It is not required, and it does not build muscle on its own — training does. If you buy one, choose a plain product with a short ingredient list from a reputable brand, and be wary of products promising steroid-like results. Those claims are marketing, and some products are spiked. Food first, always.

Myths to drop

  • "You must eat protein within 30 minutes of training." The "anabolic window" is far wider than marketed. Total daily protein and a meal within a few hours of training is fine.
  • "More protein = more muscle." Beyond about 2.0 g/kg, extra protein does not add muscle. Training, calories, sleep, and consistency do.
  • "Protein damages healthy kidneys." In healthy people, protein at the ranges above is not shown to harm kidneys. People with kidney disease need medical guidance.
  • "Vegetarians cannot build muscle." They can. Protein planning and progressive training matter, not the source of the protein.

Frequently asked questions

How much protein do I need per day?

If you train regularly, roughly 1.4–2.0 grams per kilogram of body weight per day. Sedentary adults generally need around 0.8–1.0 grams per kilogram.

Can vegetarians get enough protein?

Yes. Lentils, chickpeas, soy, paneer, curd, milk, eggs, nuts, and seeds cover protein needs when meals are planned. Variety across the day matters more than any single food.

Is protein powder necessary?

No. Protein powder is a convenience food, not a requirement. If you reach your protein target from meals, you do not need it.

Is too much protein harmful?

Very high intakes are unnecessary and can crowd out other nutrients. People with kidney disease or other medical conditions should follow their doctor's guidance.
