What Haircut Should I Get, and What Do I Tell My Barber?

By Lookauramax Team · Last updated: 26 Oct 2026 · General information, not medical advice.

Pick by your hair, then hand your barber a brief. That is the whole method: a style that fits your hair type, your texture, and the upkeep you will actually do — and a card with the style name, sides, top length, finish, neckline, and next cut. No guessing in the chair. The rest of this guide shows how to choose, what each barber term means, and exactly what goes on the card.

Start with your hair, not someone else's photo

The match starts with your hair, not with a photo of someone else. A photo can show a style you like, but it cannot show whether your hair will hold that shape. Three things decide more than any photo:

  • Texture. Straight hair lies flat and shows every line of a cut. Wavy hair bends and adds volume on its own. Curly hair springs up as it dries, so it sits shorter dry than it looks wet. Coily hair shrinks the most and needs length kept to hold its shape.
  • Thickness and density. Thick hair has weight to remove and shape to manage. Fine hair loses body fast if too much is thinned out, so it needs a lighter hand. Knowing which one you have stops the most common mistake: asking for a cut built for the opposite hair.
  • Growth patterns. Cowlicks, a widow's peak, a crown that swirls one way — these are not flaws, they are directions your hair already moves in. A cut that fights them needs daily styling to hold. A cut that works with them mostly behaves on its own.

Two honest notes before you pick. First, thinning and receding hair are not fixed by a haircut: a shorter, textured cut can make thinning less visible, but shedding that worries you is a doctor matter, not a barber matter. Second, you will see plenty of "which cut fits your face shape" quizzes online. We do not score faces — a cut is chosen by hair and upkeep, and your barber's eyes settle the rest.

If you do not know your hair type, that is normal. Our free hair type quiz takes a minute, and the answer changes which styles suit you. It asks about texture, thickness, and how your hair behaves — the same three things above.

Treat the shortlist as a starting point, not a verdict. No match can promise a cut will suit you — only you and your barber, looking at your actual hair, make the final call. What the Match does is remove the worst options before you sit in the chair.

Five styles that cover most men

Most men's haircuts are variations on a few shapes. Each one below says plainly which hair it fits and what it asks of you after.

  • Textured fringe. Three to four inches on top, cut with texture so the front breaks into pieces, with tapered sides. Fits straight, wavy, and curly hair. Upkeep: low to medium — a little product, pushed forward or swept aside. Fine hair needs a light hand on thinning.
  • Low taper. The sides stay full and only the edges — sideburns, around the ears, the neckline — are tapered short. Fits almost every hair type, including hair that is thinning, because it keeps coverage where it matters. Upkeep: a clean-up every three to four weeks keeps it sharp.
  • Classic side part. Longer on top, combed to one side, with neat tapered or scissor-cut sides. Fits straight and wavy hair best, and suits men who want a tidy, grown-up shape. Upkeep: medium — it needs a comb and a little product most mornings.
  • Short crop. Short all over, with a short fringe and faded or tapered sides. Fits thick and wavy hair well, and men who want almost no styling. Upkeep: the lowest of the five — wash, dry, done — with a cut every three to four weeks.
  • Longer flow. Length kept through the top and sides, worn swept back or loose. Fits wavy, curly, and thick straight hair that has the weight to hold it. Upkeep: the highest — conditioning, drying, and patience through the growing stage.

If none of these sounds like you, the brief below matters even more: the barber needs your words, not a style name.

Barber terms, plainly

Most bad haircuts are communication failures, not skill failures. "Short" means different lengths to different people, so barbers work in numbers and names. These are the ones that matter:

  • Guard numbers. Clipper guards leave a set length: number 0 is near scalp (about 1.5 mm), number 1 is about 3 mm, number 2 is about 6 mm, number 3 is about 10 mm, and number 4 is about 13 mm. A higher number leaves more hair. Saying "number 2 on the sides" removes all doubt about what "short" means.
  • Fade. The sides blend from very short at the bottom to longer higher up, with no visible line. A low fade starts the blend near the ears; a mid fade starts around the middle of the ear; a high fade climbs higher and removes more weight. "Faded to skin" means the bottom is shaved bare — say it only if you mean it.
  • Taper. Like a fade, but gentler and lower: only the edges are shortened, and the sides keep most of their length. A taper kept clean stays sharp for longer, and it is the safer choice when you are unsure about a fade.
  • Blended vs disconnected. Blended means the top flows into the sides with no hard line. Disconnected means there is a visible step between a longer top and shorter sides — a deliberate, sharper look, not a mistake.
  • Textured / point cut. The barber cuts into the ends at an angle so the hair breaks into pieces instead of sitting as one solid block. This is what makes a fringe look lived-in rather than helmet-like.
  • Neckline. Tapered means the nape fades out naturally; blocked (or squared) means it is cut in a straight line. Tapered grows out more softly. Blocked looks crisper on day one and harsher as it grows.

One confirming question prevents most surprises: before the clippers start, ask, "When you say fade, do you mean to skin?" Terms vary between shops, and ten seconds of confirming beats three weeks of growing out.

The Barber Brief: what goes on the card

The Barber Brief is the second half of the feature: it turns your pick into a card you can show your barber. Hand the brief over at the start of the appointment and talk through it. A good brief holds seven lines, each one answering a question the barber would otherwise have to guess:

  1. Style name + reference. The Library style and one photo of it. The photo shows the shape; the words below it make the shape exact.
  2. Sides. Guard number and blend: for example, "number 2, low taper, blended, not to skin."
  3. Top length. Inches, not "long": "three to four inches on top."
  4. Finish. Textured, blunt, or layered — how the ends should sit.
  5. Neckline. Tapered or blocked, and how clean around the ears.
  6. Styling. How you will wear it day to day — pushed forward, swept aside, natural dry — so the cut is shaped for real mornings, not the chair.
  7. Next cut. The cadence that keeps it: three to four weeks for a taper or fade, longer for length.

An example brief, whole: "Textured fringe. Sides: number 2, low taper, blended, not to skin. Top: three to four inches, textured. Neckline: tapered. Wear: pushed forward, natural dry. Next cut: four weeks." Read the top line aloud, show the photo, then let the barber ask questions. If they suggest a change, listen: that is exactly the judgement a card cannot replace.

How often to go back

Cadence is part of the cut. A taper or fade loses its edge in three to four weeks as the blend grows out; medium styles hold four to six weeks; longer styles stretch to six to eight or more, because length hides growth. Growing hair out still needs shape trims every six to eight weeks, or the shape goes before the length arrives. Most of a good haircut is decided before the clippers come out: the style, the brief, and the date you come back.

If you hate the cut

It happens, even with a good brief. Do not panic-cut it shorter the same day — shorter is the one direction you cannot undo. Live with it for a few days first: hair settles, and a cut that looks severe wet often softens dry. If you still dislike it, write down exactly what to change — "sides too short", "top too blunt", "fringe too heavy" — and take that list to the next cut instead of a vague "fix it". A barber who knows what went wrong can correct it.

Honest limits and myths

"A photo of another man promises the same result." No. His hair, growth patterns, and styling routine are not yours. A photo is a direction, not a promise — the brief exists because words plus a photo beat either one alone.

"There is one perfect cut for you." No such cut exists. There are cuts that fit your hair and your upkeep, and cuts that fight them. Between two fitting cuts, the better one is the one you will actually maintain.

"Shorter always looks tidier." Not for thinning hair or strong cowlicks, where very short sides can expose what you wanted covered, or let a cowlick stand up. Coverage and direction matter more than length alone.

"A matching feature paints the cut onto your photo." Haircut Match does not do that. It matches Library styles to your hair inputs and writes the brief. Anything that claims to paint a cut onto your photo is a different product making a different promise, and this post does not make it.

"Trending cuts suit everyone." A trending cut suits the hair it was built for. The textured fringe is everywhere right now because it fits a lot of hair — not because it fits all of it. If your hair will not hold it without daily work you will not do, it is the wrong cut for you, however popular it is.

Frequently asked questions

What should I tell my barber for a fade?

Name three things: how short the sides go, where the blend starts, and whether it goes to skin. A clear version sounds like this: “Number 2 on the sides, blended up from the middle of the ear, not to skin.” If you do want it to skin, say so — “faded to skin” and “blended to a number 2” are different cuts, and your barber cannot guess which one you meant. Bring one photo of the blend you like, and ask one confirming question before the clippers start: “When you say fade, do you mean to skin?”

What haircut should I get for curly hair?

Keep length on top and cut with shrinkage in mind: curly hair sits shorter dry than it looks wet, so a cut that looks right in the chair can spring up shorter at home. Ask for layers that remove weight without thinning the hair aggressively, and keep the sides long enough that the curl keeps its shape instead of puffing out. A textured crop or a longer curly top with tapered sides suits most curly hair, and the upkeep is washing, conditioning, and letting it dry naturally more often than brushing it straight.

What should I tell my barber when growing my hair out?

Say the goal first — “I am growing it out” — then what to tidy and what to leave alone. A growing-out brief usually reads: trim the split ends only, keep the shape, clean the neckline and around the ears, and do not take length off the top. Without that second half, a normal tidy-up can remove the very length you spent months growing. Come back every six to eight weeks for shape trims while it grows, and expect an awkward middle stage: that is normal, and it passes.

How do I ask my barber for a textured fringe?

Ask for three to four inches kept on top, cut with texture so the front breaks into pieces instead of sitting as one solid block, with tapered sides to keep the edges clean. Say how you wear the fringe — pushed forward, swept to one side, or messy — because the same cut behaves differently each way. If your hair is fine, add one line: do not thin it aggressively, since fine hair loses its body fast when too much weight comes out.
