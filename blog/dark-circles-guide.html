Dark Circles: What Actually Helps

By Lookauramax Team · Published 17 Oct 2026 · General information, not medical advice.

Dark circles have more than one cause — and the cause decides the fix. Here is how to tell which kind you have, what genuinely helps, and which products are a waste of money.

Dark circles are one of the most complained-about face concerns, and one of the most misunderstood. Most advice treats them as a single problem with a single fix (usually an expensive eye cream). In reality, "dark circles" is an umbrella for at least four different things that look similar in the mirror but need different solutions — and two of them barely respond to creams at all. The good news: once you know which kind you have, the honest plan is simple, cheap, and actually works.

Figure out which kind you have

  • Vascular (bluish/purple). The skin under the eyes is the thinnest on the face, so blood vessels show through. Looks bluish or purple, worse when you are tired, pale, or dehydrated. If you gently stretch the under-eye skin and the colour stays the same, it is likely vascular.
  • Pigmentation (brownish). Extra melanin in the under-eye area, more common in medium and deeper skin tones and with sun exposure. Looks brown, does not change much when you stretch the skin, and often runs in families.
  • Structural shadows. Not darkness in the skin at all — a shadow cast by an under-eye hollow, tear trough, or puffiness above it. Looks worse in overhead or harsh lighting and better in soft, front-facing light. Creams cannot fix a shadow.
  • Fluid and puffiness. Poor sleep, salty late-night meals, alcohol, allergies, and crying all cause fluid to pool under the eyes, making the area look swollen and darker. Usually worst in the morning and better by afternoon.

Most people have a mix — commonly vascular plus a little puffiness, or pigmentation plus a hollow. That is why single-product fixes disappoint: a cream aimed at pigmentation does nothing for a shadow.

What actually helps (by cause)

For all kinds

  • Sleep. Consistent 7–9 hours. Sleep deprivation makes every kind of dark circle look worse — paler skin shows vessels more, fluid pools, and the whole face looks tired. It will not erase genetic pigmentation, but it is the highest-value free fix.
  • Daily sunscreen. UV deepens pigmentation and thins skin over time. Sunscreen around the eyes (a gentle mineral formula if your eyes sting) prevents the brownish kind from getting worse. This is the one step people skip and then wonder why nothing improves.
  • Gentle care. No rubbing, no pulling, no harsh scrubs around the eyes. The under-eye skin is thin and easy to damage. Pat products on with your ring finger. Remove makeup gently.
  • Allergies. Rubbing itchy, allergic eyes ("allergic shiners") darkens the area over time. Treating the allergy — with guidance from a doctor or pharmacist — helps more than any eye cream.

For vascular circles

Cold compresses (a chilled spoon, a cold gel mask, or a damp cloth) for 5–10 minutes constrict vessels and reduce puffiness temporarily. Caffeine-containing eye products can have a mild, temporary tightening effect. Staying hydrated and sleeping well reduce how visible vessels look. A vitamin C serum can support skin over time, but expect modest change, not transformation.

For pigmentation

Sun protection matters most. Ingredients with real evidence for pigmentation include vitamin C, niacinamide, azelaic acid, and retinoids — used gently, one at a time, with sunscreen. Results take 8–12 weeks. Be patient and stop anything that irritates the delicate eye area. Avoid any product promising to "whiten" or "lighten" skin — those claims are a red flag, and harsh lightening products can damage skin. Lookauramax never recommends skin whitening or lightening, and neither should you.

For structural shadows

Honesty first: no cream, serum, or home remedy fills a hollow or removes a shadow. What helps: good lighting (face a window for photos), adequate sleep, and keeping the area moisturised so the skin looks smooth. In-office options exist (a dermatologist can discuss them), but they are medical procedures with real costs and risks — not something to decide from an advertisement. If you consider them, get an in-person consultation with a qualified dermatologist.

For puffiness

Sleep on your back with your head slightly raised, limit very salty late-night meals and alcohol, manage allergies, and use a cold compress in the morning. Puffiness that is new, severe, one-sided, or painful needs a doctor, not a cream.

What is a waste of money

  • Expensive eye creams with miracle claims. A basic, fragrance-free moisturiser does nearly everything an eye cream does. "Instantly erases dark circles" is marketing, not dermatology.
  • DIY remedies with no evidence. Potato slices, tea bags, and cucumber feel nice and cool the area, which can temporarily reduce puffiness — but they do not treat pigmentation or hollows. Enjoy them as a cold compress, not a cure.
  • Colour-correcting as a "treatment". Concealer is a fine cosmetic fix and there is nothing wrong with using it. Just know it hides the circle; it does not treat it.
  • Stacking five under-eye products. The eye area reacts badly to overload. One gentle product, used consistently, beats a shelf of them.

A realistic 4-week plan

  1. Fix sleep: same bedtime and wake time, screens off 30 minutes before bed.
  2. Sunscreen every morning, including around the eyes.
  3. One gentle under-eye product (moisturiser or a vitamin C / niacinamide formula), morning and night, patted on — no rubbing.
  4. Cold compress on puffy mornings, 5–10 minutes.
  5. Take a photo in the same light once a week. Judge after a month, not after a day.

If nothing has changed after 8–12 weeks of steady care, or if circles appeared suddenly, a dermatologist can identify the cause and discuss options that products cannot offer. That is a sensible next step, not a defeat.

Frequently asked questions

What causes dark circles under the eyes?

Dark circles have several causes: thin skin showing blood vessels, pigmentation, shadows from under-eye hollows, fluid retention, sun damage, and genetics. The cause decides what helps, which is why one cream does not fix every case.

Can eye creams remove dark circles?

Eye creams can help mild puffiness and dryness and slightly improve tone over time, but no cream removes genetic pigmentation or structural hollows. Be sceptical of any cream promising dramatic or instant results.

Does sleep fix dark circles?

Sleep helps the kind of dark circles caused by tiredness, fluid retention, and paleness — they look worse when you are sleep-deprived. It does not erase genetic pigmentation or deep hollows.

When should I see a doctor about dark circles?

See a doctor if dark circles appeared suddenly, come with swelling, pain, vision changes, or other symptoms, or if they are severely affecting you. A dermatologist can identify the cause and discuss prescription or in-office options.
