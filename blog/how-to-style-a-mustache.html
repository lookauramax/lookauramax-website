How to Style a Mustache

By Lookauramax Team · Last updated: 8 Nov 2026 · General information, not medical advice.

Styling a mustache is mostly combing in the right direction, plus a little wax when the shape needs it. That is the whole job: pick a style your growth can actually support, comb it into shape every morning, and use wax only where the hair will not stay on its own. Most bad mustache days come from two mistakes: wax before the length is there, and far too much of it.

Pick your style first

The style decides the work. A mustache that only needs a comb asks for two minutes a morning. A handlebar asks for wax, training, and patience every day for months. Choose by the hair you can grow and the effort you will honestly give it.

Natural styles — chevron, walrus, lampshade. These follow the shape of your upper lip and need no wax at all. A chevron is the place to start: two to three months to full thickness, hiding uneven growth better than any other style. A walrus is the same idea left to grow — six months to a year, over the lip. A lampshade is a chevron trimmed into a straight-edged trapezoid. One comb and a weekly trim covers them all.

Thin styles — the pencil. A thin line a few millimetres tall, worn just above the lip. It takes two to four weeks to show, and suits darker hair best, because the line needs to read clearly. The upkeep is the opposite of the chevron: the shaved band above the line needs a razor every day or two, and the bottom edge needs the same attention. Highest maintenance, least hair.

Waxed styles — handlebar, English, Hungarian. These have shaped ends, and they are the only styles that truly need wax. A handlebar takes three to six months before the ends are long enough to curl, and needs wax and training every day. The English is long and narrow, ends combed to points and waxed daily. The Hungarian is the biggest of the family, sitting lower on the cheeks with a natural curl. If you will not style it every morning, do not start one.

Start with a chevron: least training, hides unevenness, and every other style can be cut out of it later. Grow first, decide later.

What you need

You need less kit than you think. A fine-tooth mustache comb is the one tool every style needs: it sets the direction, spreads the wax, and shows the true shape first. A small tin of mustache wax is next, but only if your style has shaped ends. For a chevron or a walrus, a drop of beard oil keeps the hair tidy. Our beard-oil guide covers the amount: one drop, warmed between your fingertips, combed down and out.

Scissors or a trimmer handle the edges — our trimming guide covers which one suits which style. A hairdryer has one job: warming wax so it spreads, then locking a curled shape with cool air. You do not need a special brush or a shelf of products. The comb and the wax do the work; the rest is patience.

The daily routine

Style it in the morning, on clean, completely dry hair. Wax will not hold on wet or oily hair, so wash first if it needs it, dry it fully, and keep the oil off until the wax has set. Five minutes, same order every day.

  1. Comb it down. Run the fine-tooth comb from the centre outward, down toward the lip and then out toward the corners. Comb it the same way every morning and the hair starts to sit that way on its own — that is training.
  2. Take a little wax. Scrape about a grain of rice of wax from the tin with your thumbnail — enough for most mustaches. Rub it between thumb and forefinger until soft. If it stays crumbly, it is not warm enough yet; warm it longer. Never add more wax to fix stiff wax.
  3. Work it in from the centre out. Start at the middle of your lip and work toward one tip, then the other side the same way. Press the wax into the hair with thumb and forefinger, not onto it. A thin, even layer holds better than a thick one, and you can always add a second pass.
  4. Comb it through. Comb through once more, centre out, to spread the wax and pull out any clumps. If the comb drags, there is too much wax in that spot — comb it out rather than adding more.
  5. Shape it with your fingers. While the wax is still soft, push the hair where you want it: down and out for a chevron, to the sides for a handlebar, ends pointed for an English. For curled ends, wrap the tip around your finger for fifteen seconds.
  6. Let it set. Leave it alone for a few minutes. The wax firms up as it cools, and the shape locks in. Do not keep touching it — every touch after it sets breaks the hold a little.

The handlebar, step by step

A handlebar is three things in order: training, wax, and the curl. Skip the training and the wax has to fight the hair all day — it will lose.

Training takes weeks, not days. Every morning and evening, sweep the corners upward with thumb and forefinger, curling the ends into place. After a few weeks the hair holds the curl on its own. Then the wax only refines a shape that is already there — it stops forcing one.

Then the wax, with a stronger hold than a chevron needs. Warm it well — a hairdryer on the tin for a few seconds makes stiff wax workable — and work it through the mustache, centre out, with extra at the ends. Smooth each handle to a point, pulling them straight out to check they match.

Then the curl. Wrap each end around your finger for fifteen seconds; a pencil gives a tighter curl. Warm air along the curl makes the wax soft enough to shape, and cool air afterwards locks it. Leave it alone for ten minutes. If it droops by lunchtime, the wax was too cold — warm it properly next time, and add a second pass instead of a bigger first one.

A handlebar takes three to six months before the ends are long enough to curl — grow a chevron first.

Styling without wax

Most mustaches never need wax. A chevron, a walrus, a lampshade, and a beardstache are all styled with a comb and, if the hair is dry, a drop of beard oil. Comb it down and out every morning, trim the lip line when it creeps into your mouth, and that is the routine. Wax adds hold, not neatness.

One habit helps without any product: towel-dry training. After a shower, towel the mustache dry by dragging the towel in the direction you want the hair to sit — centre out, down and to the sides. Warm, damp hair is at its most willing, and drying it that way every day teaches it to fall that way.

What no-wax styling cannot do is hold a shaped end. A handlebar curl, an English point, a swept-up tip — all of those need wax, and they need it every day. The style you will actually keep beats the one you style twice and give up on.

The gap in the middle

A gap in the middle is the most common styling worry, and it is usually a growth problem, not a styling one. The middle of the lip is often the thinnest part, and the hair there is slower to fill in. Time is the honest fix: a chevron grown for two to three months covers far more than a short one.

While it grows, comb the hair across the gap instead of away from it, from the fuller side toward the thin middle, and use a little wax to hold the hairs over it.

What does not help: cutting the mustache shorter to match the gap, which only makes the whole thing thinner, or piling wax into the gap, which clumps and draws the eye. No product grows hair where the follicles are thin — that is genetics. Our beard-growth guide says the same about patchy beards: keep it shorter, keep the edges clean, and give it the full growth before deciding.

Five mistakes that waste the wax

Too much wax. A big scrape looks like it will hold better; it holds worse. Thick wax clumps, feels crunchy, and shows white in the hair. Start with a grain of rice.

Wax on wet or oily hair. Wax will not bind to damp hair, and oil under wax kills the hold. Dry the mustache fully, keep the beard oil off until the wax has set, and wash it first if yesterday's product is still in it.

Styling before the length is there. A handlebar needs ends long enough to curl, and a chevron needs two to three months before it has the thickness to look like one. A two-week mustache cannot hold a shape yet. That is not a technique problem. It is a calendar problem. Grow first.

Cutting the ends you need. Trimming before you have decided the style is the same job in the wrong order. Decide the style, then trim around it — and never run a guard over a handlebar. The ends are the style. The rule: scissors only, lip line only, for any long style.

Touching it all day. Wax sets as it cools. Every re-shape after it sets breaks the hold a little, and by the afternoon there is nothing left to hold. Shape it once in the morning, let it set, and leave it alone.

When it will not sit right

Some mustaches fight back, and the cause is usually not the styling. A mustache that curls up at the ends is doing what facial hair does — the hair is coarse and the growth points outward. Our curly-beard guide explains the same thing for beards: the curl comes from the shape of the follicle, and no combing straightens it. Train the curl into the shape you want, or trim the ends short. More wax only makes it crunchy.

A mustache that will not connect to the beard is the same genetics story as the gap: some men grow a full join and some do not. A shorter, tidier mustache with clean edges looks deliberate even with modest density.

If the mustache keeps catching food and drink, clean the lip line and take the bulk down a little — a shorter mustache that stays out of the way beats a long one you are always wiping.

And if the skin under the mustache itches or stays red, the problem is the skin, not the hair. Wash the mustache, moisturise the skin beneath it, and leave it alone for a few days.

Frequently asked questions

How do you style a mustache with wax?

Start with a clean, fully dry mustache. Scrape about a grain of rice of wax with your thumbnail, warm it between thumb and forefinger until soft, work it in from the centre out to the tips, comb it through, then shape with your fingers and let it set for a few minutes.

Can you style a mustache without wax?

Yes, for most styles. A chevron or walrus needs only a comb and a drop of beard oil. Wax is only needed for styles with shaped ends, like a handlebar or an English — without it those ends will not hold.

How do you style a mustache with a gap in the middle?

Give it time first: most gaps are thin growth, not a styling problem, and a chevron grown for two to three months covers more than a short one. Comb the hair across the gap, not away from it, and use a little wax to hold the hairs over it. No product fills a gap on its own.

How do you style a mustache with a goatee?

Treat them as one shape: comb the mustache down and out so it meets the chin hair, keep the line where they join clean, and wax only the mustache ends. If the mustache is much longer than the goatee, the join looks disconnected — trim the mustache to the goatee's length.
