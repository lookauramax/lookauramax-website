How to Talk to Guys: Making Male Friends as an Adult

Talking to other men as an adult can feel harder than it should — at the gym, at work, at a party, most men were never actually taught how. This guide covers the honest way to start conversations with guys, keep them going, and turn small talk into real friendship: read the room, ask real questions, listen properly, and follow up like you mean it.

What is talking to guys, as an adult?

Talking to guys, as an adult man, means starting and holding natural conversations with other men — at the gym, at work, in sports and social settings — with the aim of connection and friendship. It is a learnable social skill, not a personality trait: open with something you both share, ask questions you actually care about, listen to the answers, and let familiarity grow over repeated meetings.

If starting a conversation with another man feels awkward, nothing is wrong with you. Many men reach adulthood with a small circle — or none — because the places that used to supply friends for free, school and college, are gone. Conversation with other men is a skill, and like any skill it improves with honest practice.

Why it feels harder than it used to

As a boy, friendship happened almost by itself. You saw the same people every day, and familiarity did the work. Adult life removes that structure: work goes remote or heads-down, people move cities, evenings fill up. Nobody is assigned a desk next to yours any more.

There is another difference. A lot of male friendship is built side by side — training, playing, building, watching something together — rather than face to face over long talks. That is not a flaw; it is a style. It means the best place to talk to guys is usually around a shared activity, where the conversation has something to stand on. Our guide to making friends as a young adult covers how to rebuild that regular contact, step by step.

Start with the shared situation

The most natural opener is the one already in front of you: something you both share. The session you are both in, the match you are both watching, the project you are both on. A comment or question about that shared thing feels natural because it is natural — it asks for no performance from either of you.

For example: “How long have you been training here?” or “What did you make of that last review?” Then listen to the answer and respond to what he actually said. The opener is ten percent of the conversation; the other ninety is what you do with the reply. If he answers fully and asks something back, the conversation is alive. If he gives one word and turns away, that is information too — the last section of this guide covers reading it well.

Talking at the gym

The gym is one of the best places to meet other men — you see the same faces at the same hours — and one of the easiest places to get it wrong. The rules are clear. Keep it brief. Talk between sets, never during one. Headphones in means not open to conversation; respect that the way you would want it respected.

Good gym openers are practical: asking for a spot, asking how he finds a particular exercise, or a short comment about training as you both finish. Keep early chats to a sentence or two. Familiarity at the gym builds over weeks of nods and short exchanges, and that slow build is normal — it is how most gym friendships actually start.

Talking at work

Work gives you shared ground for free: the same projects, deadlines, and frustrations. Use the natural gaps — coffee, lunch, the walk between meetings — rather than interrupting deep focus. Ask about his role, his side of a project, how he got into the field. People speak easily about work they know well.

Keep workplace conversation clean of gossip. Talking about absent colleagues to win closeness teaches everyone listening that you cannot be trusted with them either. Friendly and professional are not opposites; the men who are easiest to talk to at work are usually both. If a lunch conversation goes well, make it a regular thing — a recurring lunch is repetition, and repetition is how colleagues become friends.

Talking in social settings

Parties, matches, hobby groups, and friends of friends are built for this. A shared event gives you a topic, a time limit, and an easy exit for both sides. Being introduced by a mutual friend is the warmest start of all, so use it: ask friends to bring you along, and say yes to invitations even when staying home feels easier.

In a group, do not hunt for the most impressive person in the room. Talk to whoever is next to you, include people standing at the edge of the group, and move around. Short, friendly exchanges with several people teach you more — and feel better — than one long conversation you treat as a performance.

Keep the conversation going

Conversations die when questions run out, and questions run out when they are closed and generic. Open questions — ones that cannot be answered with yes or no — give the other man something to work with. “What got you into that?” beats “Do you like it?” every time. Follow-ups matter even more: respond to the detail he just gave you, not to the next question on your list.

Share something back. A conversation where one man only asks questions feels like an interview, and one where he only talks about himself feels like a lecture. Trade: a view, a small story, a genuine reaction. You do not need to agree with everything he says — honest, friendly disagreement is often where a conversation gets interesting.

Listen like you mean it

Most people think good talkers are good speakers. Mostly, they are good listeners. Put the phone away. Make comfortable eye contact. Remember what he told you — his sport, his project, his kid’s name — and ask about it next time. Being remembered is rare, and people notice it.

Listening also tells you whether the conversation is working. Full answers, questions back, and turned-toward body language mean he is in. Short answers and drifting attention mean he is not — and that is fine. The next section covers the step that turns good conversations into actual friendships.

From small talk to actual friendship

Here is the step most men skip. A good conversation is not yet a friendship, and most potential friendships quietly die because neither man suggests meeting again. Both enjoyed talking; both assumed the other was not that interested. Do not let that be you. Before you part, take one small initiative: “A few of us watch the match on Sundays, if you want in,” or “I train here Tuesdays and Thursdays — say hello next time.”

Then follow through. Send the message. Suggest the time. Show up. If he declines or goes quiet, accept it without resentment — schedules and seasons differ, and a polite no costs nobody anything. Read the room, and respect a no

Not everyone wants to talk at every moment, and that is not rejection — it is life. Headphones in, eyes down, one-word answers, turning away: these are clear signals that a man is not open to conversation right now. The strong response is a smile, a “no problem,” and leaving him to it.

The same applies to messages. One friendly message is enough; if it goes unanswered, do not chase it on another platform or through mutual friends. Nobody owes you their time, and respecting that is part of what makes you someone other men are glad to talk to when the moment is right.

Frequently asked questions

How do I start a conversation with a guy I don't know?

Start with the shared situation — the gym session, the match, the event you are both at — with a comment or a question about it. Then listen to the answer and respond to what he actually said. The opener is ten percent of a conversation; listening is the rest. If he answers fully and asks something back, keep going. If he gives one word and turns away, smile and leave it there.

How do men make friends as adults?

Through repetition and one small act of initiative. Join something regular — a sport, a class, a training schedule, a hobby group — show up at the same time each week, and talk to the same men repeatedly. When a conversation goes well, suggest a concrete next meeting. Most adult male friendships exist because one man suggested a second meeting instead of hoping it would happen by itself.

What should I talk about with other guys?

Start with what you share: training, work, sport, the event you are both at, plans you both have. Ask open questions about his experience of those things, follow up on the details he gives you, and share something real in return. Avoid an interrogation on one side and a monologue on the other — a good conversation trades.

What if he doesn't seem interested in talking?

Accept the signal with grace. Short answers, no eye contact, headphones in, or turning away mean he is not open to conversation right now. Say “no problem,” leave it there, and do not follow up with more messages or try again through other people. A no to a chat is about the moment, not about your worth — and respecting it keeps the door open for another day.
