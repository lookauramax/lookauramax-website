Sunscreen: The Honest Guide to Daily Sun Protection

By Lookauramax Team · Last updated: 18 Oct 2026 · General information, not medical advice.

If you do one skincare step for the rest of your life, make it sunscreen. It prevents uneven tone, dark marks, premature ageing, and skin cancer — and it costs less than any serum.

What is sunscreen?

Sunscreen is a product that protects skin from UV rays. Broad-spectrum sunscreen covers UVA and UVB rays. Daily use protects against sunburn and long-term skin damage. It is one of the most effective long-term skin habits. Apply it every morning, even on cloudy days.

Sunscreen has a strange reputation. Some people treat it as a beach-only product. Others worry it is greasy, causes breakouts, or is full of chemicals. The reality is clearr: daily sunscreen is the single most proven, highest-value skincare habit that exists. Dermatologists agree on almost nothing — but they agree on this. Sun damage is cumulative. Every unprotected hour adds up over years, showing as uneven tone, dark spots, fine lines, and sagging. And unlike most skincare claims, this one is backed by decades of evidence, not marketing.

This guide tells you how to pick a sunscreen you will actually wear, how much to use, when to reapply, and which myths to ignore. The best sunscreen is not the most expensive one. It is the one you put on every morning without thinking about it.

Why daily sunscreen matters

Sunlight contains two types of ultraviolet radiation that damage skin. UVB causes sunburn and plays a major role in skin cancer. UVA penetrates deeper, causes most of the visible ageing — wrinkles, sagging, uneven tone — and is present all day, all year, even through clouds and window glass. A broad-spectrum sunscreen protects against both. Daily use keeps skin tone even, prevents dark marks from getting darker, slows visible ageing, and reduces skin cancer risk.

It is worth saying plainly: sunscreen is for every skin tone. Darker skin has more natural protection against sunburn, but it still gets sun damage, uneven tone, dark marks, and skin cancer — and damage is often diagnosed later because people assume they do not need protection. Everyone needs daily sunscreen.

How to pick a sunscreen

  • Broad-spectrum, SPF 30 or higher. Broad-spectrum means it covers UVA and UVB. SPF 30 blocks about 97 percent of UVB rays; SPF 50 about 98 percent. Above 50, the gains are small — proper application matters far more than a bigger number.
  • A texture you will wear. Gel or fluid formulas suit oily skin. Cream formulas suit dry skin. If it pills under makeup, feels heavy, or leaves a strong white cast, you will stop using it — and a sunscreen you skip protects no one. Try a few affordable options until one feels invisible.
  • Chemical or mineral — your call. Chemical (organic) filters absorb UV; mineral (zinc oxide, titanium dioxide) filters sit on top and scatter it. Both work when used correctly. Mineral formulas suit sensitive skin but can leave more white cast; chemical formulas usually feel lighter. Pick whichever you will wear daily.
  • Water-resistant if you sweat or swim. "Water-resistant" means it holds up for 40 or 80 minutes in water — not all day. Reapply after swimming or heavy sweating regardless of the label.

The label matters — broad-spectrum, SPF 30+, a recent expiry date — not the brand.

How to use it properly

  1. How much: about two finger-lengths of product for face and neck — a strip along your index and middle fingers. Most people use a quarter of what they need, which cuts protection dramatically.
  2. Where: face, neck, ears, and the back of the neck. These are the spots people forget and where damage shows first. If you wear short sleeves, cover exposed arms too.
  3. When: every morning, as the last step of your skincare routine, before makeup. Apply 15 minutes before going outside if you can.
  4. Reapply: every two hours of continuous sun exposure, and after swimming, heavy sweating, or towelling off. For a normal indoor day, one morning application is the baseline — reapply if you spend your lunch break or commute in strong sun.
  5. Do not mix it into moisturiser. Mixing sunscreen with moisturiser or foundation dilutes it. Layer it: moisturiser first, sunscreen on top, makeup last.

The myths that keep people unprotected

  • "I don't need it on cloudy days." Up to 80 percent of UV rays pass through clouds. Cloudy-day damage is real and cumulative.
  • "I don't need it indoors." UVA passes through window glass. If you sit near a bright window, drive a lot, or work by glass, daily sunscreen still matters.
  • "My makeup has SPF, so I'm covered." Makeup with SPF helps, but almost nobody applies enough foundation to reach the labelled protection. Treat it as a bonus, not a replacement.
  • "Sunscreen causes breakouts." Some formulas can clog pores, but many are labelled non-comedogenic and oil-free. If one breaks you out, switch formulas rather than quitting sunscreen.
  • "I have dark skin, so I don't need it." Darker skin burns less but still takes sun damage and still gets skin cancer. Daily protection applies to everyone.
  • "Sunscreen is toxic." Sunscreens approved by regulators have been tested for safety at normal use. The known risks of unprotected sun exposure — skin cancer, burns, premature ageing — are far larger than any theoretical concern about approved filters. If you prefer, mineral formulas are a well-studied alternative.

When sunscreen is not enough

Sunscreen is one layer, not a force field. In strong sun, add shade, a hat, and sunglasses — your eyes and the skin around them need protection too. Avoid tanning beds entirely: they deliver concentrated UV and accelerate ageing and cancer risk. If you notice a mole that changes shape, colour, or size, bleeds, or looks new and unusual, see a dermatologist promptly. Frequently asked questions

Do I need sunscreen on cloudy days?

Yes. Up to 80 percent of UV rays pass through clouds. Daily sunscreen matters on cloudy days, in winter, and indoors near large windows.

How much sunscreen should I use on my face?

About two finger-lengths of product for the face and neck — a strip along the index and middle fingers. Most people use far too little, which cuts real protection.

Is SPF 50 much better than SPF 30?

SPF 30 blocks about 97 percent of UVB rays and SPF 50 about 98 percent. The bigger difference is how much you apply and whether you reapply. SPF 30 used properly beats SPF 50 used thinly.

Does sunscreen cause vitamin D deficiency?

Normal daily sunscreen use has not been shown to cause vitamin D deficiency in most people. If you are concerned, a blood test and a doctor's advice beat skipping sun protection.
