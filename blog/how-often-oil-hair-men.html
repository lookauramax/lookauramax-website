How Often Should Men Oil Their Hair

By Lookauramax Team · Last updated: 4 Nov 2026 · General information, not medical advice.

Once or twice a week, for most men. That is the honest starting point, and it covers more heads than any other number. Dry, curly, or coily hair can take up to three oilings a week. An oily scalp needs far less — once every couple of weeks, ends only, or not at all. Oil goes on before you wash, stays on for under an hour, and gets washed out properly. Get those three things right — how often, where, and washed out — and oiling does what it is actually good at: softer hair that snaps less.

What hair oil actually does

Hair oil works on the hair shaft, the dead fibre above the skin. It coats that fibre, seals in moisture, and flattens the cuticle so strands slide past each other instead of catching. That is why oiled hair combs easier and snaps less when you brush it. A 2003 laboratory study in the Journal of Cosmetic Science found coconut oil reduced protein loss from hair samples, damaged and undamaged alike, while sunflower and mineral oil did not. That is a lab finding, not a promise about your head. It does match what men notice in the mirror, though: coconut oil is the one oil with real evidence behind it as a pre-wash treatment.

The limits matter just as much as the benefits. It does not make hair grow faster. Growth happens in the follicle, under the skin, and no oil reaches it — our hair-fall guide says the same about every oil on the shelf. It does not repair damage from inside, and it does not glue a split end back together; it makes the end look smoother for a wash or two, and that is all. If a bottle promises regrowth or repair, that is marketing, not hair care.

Think of oil as conditioner with a different texture. Our conditioning guide follows the same logic: the product answers a need the wash creates. Oiling is the heavier, slower version — best before a wash, not after one.

How often, by hair type

Your scalp and your hair type set the number. Find yours below and start there for a month before changing anything.

Fine, straight hair with an oily scalp. Once every two weeks, ends only — or skip oiling entirely. Your scalp already coats the roots, and added oil travels fast down straight hair. A full oiling here mostly buys a greasy day and flat roots.

Normal hair, straight or wavy. Once or twice a week, mid-lengths to ends, before washing. This is the straightforward case, and the one the weekly advice from dermatologists is written for.

Thick or wavy hair. Twice a week, mid-lengths to ends. Thicker hair drinks more oil before it looks coated, and the ends are older and drier than the roots. If the ends still catch after rinsing, a single drop smoothed over the ends on damp hair between washes helps.

Curly hair. Two to three times a week, along the full length. Curls lift the cuticle and lose moisture faster, and every bend in a curl is a weak point where breakage starts. Oil before washing, and a tiny amount on the ends between washes if the curls feel rough.

Coily or textured hair. Two to three times a week, generously, along the full length, plus a leave-in between washes if you use one. Natural oil travels slowest down a tight curl, so the ends of coily hair are the driest part of the head. This is the hair type that gains the most from oiling, and the one where skipping it shows first.

Very short or buzzed hair (under about 2 cm). Oiling is optional. Hair that short is cut off before dryness shows, and it does not tangle. If you like the feel, a drop rubbed over the top is plenty. If not, skipping it is not neglect.

Bleached, coloured, or heat-damaged hair. Twice a week before washing, mid-lengths to ends. Damaged hair loses moisture fastest and gains the most from a coating — but it still will not be repaired. Oil improves how damaged hair looks and handles. It does not undo the damage.

The scalp question

Here is where tradition and dermatology disagree, and you should hear both sides. Oiling the scalp is an old habit in many families, and a gentle scalp massage feels good. Dermatologists lean the other way: Cleveland Clinic's dermatology advice says to steer clear of oiling the scalp, because it can raise the risk of seborrheic dermatitis — dandruff, in plain words — and people prone to dandruff should avoid oils altogether.

The honest middle ground is this. If your scalp is healthy — no flakes, no itching, no redness — a light touch on the scalp during oiling is unlikely to hurt you, and plenty of men do it without trouble. But it is optional, not the point. The hair is what benefits. And if your scalp flakes, itches, or gets sore, keep oil off it completely until the skin settles, and read our scalp-itch guide for what the causes usually are. Oiling a flaky scalp to fix it is the one move most likely to make it worse.

One more scalp truth: oil does not feed the follicle. Massaging feels good and may briefly improve blood flow, but that is comfort, not a growth treatment. Enjoy it for what it is.

How to oil properly

Frequency is half the answer. The other half is a method that does not leave you greasy for two days.

  1. Start with dry, brushed hair. Comb or brush first so the oil spreads evenly instead of pooling in tangles. Oiling knotted hair just moves the knots around.
  2. Use a small amount. A pea-sized dab for short hair, about a teaspoon for long or thick hair. Warm it between your palms first. You will feel when it is enough: the hair turns slippery. If it looks wet, you used too much.
  3. Put it where your hair type says. Mid-lengths to ends for straight and wavy hair. Full length for dry, curly, or coily hair. Scalp only if yours is healthy and you want the massage — never if it flakes or itches.
  4. Leave it 20 minutes to an hour. That is enough for the coating to do its work. Longer is not better: oil left on for a full day collects dust and build-up, and some dermatologists warn that oil left on while you sleep can clog follicles. If you do leave it on all night, that should be the exception, and the morning wash needs to be thorough.
  5. Wash it out properly. Shampoo the scalp with your fingertips, let the rinse carry the shampoo down the lengths, and rinse lukewarm. Very heavy oilings sometimes need a second shampoo. If your hair still feels coated after rinsing, that was too much oil, not too little shampoo.
  6. Check the result, not the routine. After a month at your starting frequency, look at the ends. Softer, fewer snaps when combing, no coated feeling at the roots — the routine is working. Greasy by lunch or flakes appearing — cut back.

Too much, too little, and how to tell

Too much oiling has a clear signature: hair that goes limp, looks greasy hours after washing, and feels coated even when freshly rinsed. On an oily scalp it can also stir up flakes. The fix is simple and usually fast: stretch the gap, use less, and keep it off the scalp. Most men who think oiling "does not suit them" were simply oiling too often or too heavily.

Too little looks different: ends that catch and snap when you comb, hair that feels rough after washing, frizz that no conditioner seems to calm. If that is your hair, move up one step in frequency — from once a week to twice, say — and give it a month. Our wash-frequency guide sets the other half of the rhythm: oiling days work best as pre-wash treatments on days you were going to shampoo anyway.

Climate moves the number too. Hot, humid weather loads the scalp with sweat and oil, so the same man can need less oiling in summer than in winter, when cold air and hot showers dry the lengths out. Let the hair tell you, not the calendar.

Myths worth dropping

Myth one: daily oiling means stronger hair. It does not. Daily full oilings mostly mean build-up, and build-up means a scalp that cannot breathe properly and hair that looks limp. The men with the best oiling results are usually the ones doing it once or twice a week, not every day.

Myth two: oil grows hair. No oil, serum, or shampoo changes the growth rate. Oils reduce breakage, so hair keeps more of its length instead of snapping — that looks like growth over months, and it is worth having, but it is not new hair.

Myth three: longer is always better. Leaving oil on all night is traditional, and some men swear by it. But longer is not automatically better, and an oily scalp left oiled all night is exactly the condition flakes thrive in. Twenty minutes to an hour covers the benefit for most hair.

Myth four: natural oil cannot cause problems. Coconut, argan, almond, and sesame oils are all gentle as oils go, but any oil can clog follicles if overused, and any plant oil can irritate a sensitive scalp. Patch-test a new oil on a small area first if your skin reacts easily, and stop if itching or redness appears.

Frequently asked questions

Should men oil their hair every day?

No, not as a full oiling. Once or twice a week is enough for most men; daily oiling leaves build-up that flattens the roots and can stir up flakes on an oily scalp. Very dry or coily hair may take a single drop smoothed over the ends between washes, but that is an ends-only touch, not a full oiling.

How often should Black men oil their hair?

Texture decides, not background: coily and textured hair usually needs oiling two to three times a week, along the full length, plus a leave-in between washes if you use one. Natural oil travels slowly down a tight curl, so the ends of textured hair are the driest part and the part that benefits most from oiling.

Should men oil their hair before or after washing?

Before, as a pre-wash treatment: apply it, leave it for 20 minutes to an hour, then shampoo it out. After washing, only a tiny amount on the ends if they feel dry — a full oiling after a wash just leaves you greasy until the next one.

Should men put oil on their scalp?

Focus on the lengths and ends. Dermatology advice says to steer clear of oiling the scalp because it can raise the risk of dandruff, and men prone to dandruff should avoid oils on the scalp entirely. If your scalp is healthy, a light touch during a massage is unlikely to hurt, but it is optional — the hair is what benefits.

Disclaimer: This post is general information, not medical advice. If you have dandruff that persists after a few weeks, a sore, cracked, or inflamed scalp, or shedding that worries you, see a doctor or dermatologist — those need an in-person look, not a guide.
