How to Look Good in Photos: Angles, Light, Pose

By Lookauramax Team · Last updated: 18 Oct 2026 · General information, not medical advice.

Wondering how to look good in photos and be more photogenic? The answer is rarely a better camera or a filter. It is light, angle, framing, posture, and a relaxed expression — the five things you can control in almost any photo, from a selfie to a group shot to a professional portrait.

What does looking good in photos mean?

Looking good in photos means presenting yourself well through light, angle, posture, and expression. It starts with facing a window, holding the camera at eye level, and relaxing your shoulders. It is a skill, not a trait. Practice changes results.

Most people who say "I am not photogenic" are not unphotogenic. They have mostly been photographed in bad light, at awkward angles, while tense and unsure what to do with their face. Fix those things and the same person looks like themselves on a good day.

1. Light: the single biggest factor

Light decides more than any other factor whether a photo looks good. Soft, even light flatters almost everyone. Harsh, direct light creates strong shadows, shiny skin, and squinting.

  • Face a window. For indoor photos, stand facing a large window so soft daylight falls evenly on your face. Never sit with a bright window behind you — it turns you into a silhouette.
  • Go outside in soft light. Overcast days, open shade, and the hour after sunrise or before sunset give soft, flattering light. Midday sun directly overhead is the hardest light to work with.
  • Avoid light from directly below or above. Light from below creates unnatural shadows, and light from directly above hollows the eyes. Light from the front or slightly to the side is safest.
  • Wipe the lens. A smudged phone lens softens every photo. A quick wipe on a clean cloth makes a visible difference.

If you are taking a photo for a scan or a profile, the same rules apply: face a window, keep the light even, and avoid shadows across half your face.

2. Angle: camera height and face direction

Angle is the second biggest factor, and small changes make a big difference. The general rule is clear: camera at or slightly above eye level, face turned a little, chin level.

  • Camera height: Hold the camera at eye level or above it. Shooting from far below the chin distorts proportions. Shooting from far above can shrink the body and widen the forehead.
  • Distance: Do not hold the camera too close. An arm's length or more is better, and a self-timer with the phone propped up is better still.
  • Face direction: Turning your face about 30 to 45 degrees to one side, rather than facing the camera dead straight on, usually looks more natural.
  • Chin: Keep your chin level. A gentle technique is to push the forehead slightly forward and down — a small movement that defines the jaw area without looking strained.
  • Eyes: Look at the lens, not the screen, for a direct gaze.

3. Pose: posture and body position

Knowing how to pose is mostly about looking relaxed rather than frozen. The body reads tension immediately in a photo: stiff shoulders, locked knees, arms pressed flat against the sides.

  • Posture first: Stand tall with relaxed shoulders, weight evenly spread or slightly on one foot. Our posture guide explains how to build it day to day.
  • Angle the body: Turn your body about 45 degrees away from the camera, then turn your shoulders and face back toward it.
  • Give your hands a job: Hold something, rest a hand in a pocket with the thumb out, or lightly touch a bag strap. Avoid clenched fists.
  • Bend a little: A slight bend in one knee or one elbow stops the body looking like a plank.
  • Move between shots: Shift your weight or take a small step between shots. Movement produces more natural photos than freezing in place.

4. Expression: the relaxed, genuine look

The expression is where most photos succeed or fail. A forced, teeth-clenched smile looks tense because it is tense.

  • Breathe out before the shot. Take a breath in, then let it out gently before the photo. This relaxes the jaw, shoulders, and mouth in one movement.
  • Soften the mouth. Keep your lips gently closed or slightly parted, with the tongue resting lightly behind your teeth.
  • Think of something real. Think of something that genuinely makes you smile — a joke, a memory, a person you like.
  • Rest between shots. Let your face go neutral between photos, then smile again.

5. Framing, background, and group photos

For most portraits, frame from the chest or waist up, with a little space above your head. For full-body photos, keep the camera at waist height and leave space around the feet. Keep the background clear: a plain wall, an open street, or soft greenery.

For selfies, hold the phone at eye level or slightly above, at arm's length or more, and use the rear camera with a timer when you can — it is usually sharper than the front camera. Turn off beauty filters and heavy smoothing if you want photos that actually look like you.

For group photos, get on the same level — crouch or sit so faces are roughly aligned — and stand close to the group rather than hanging back at the edge, where wide lenses distort you. For candids, keep moving: walk, talk, laugh.

A quick pre-photo checklist

  • Light even on your face, window or shade in front of you, not behind you.
  • Lens wiped, background tidy.
  • Camera at or slightly above eye level, at arm's length or more.
  • Body angled, shoulders relaxed, hands with something to do.
  • Take several shots and pick the best — even professionals do.

If you want to look better in photos over time rather than in one shot, the longer-term work is the same as any honest glow-up: grooming, posture, sleep, and clothes that fit. Our guide to looking good covers that side of it.

Frequently asked questions

How can I look good in photos?

Use soft, even light on your face, keep the camera at or slightly above eye level, stand with relaxed upright posture, and use a natural expression. Good light and a relaxed pose matter far more than the camera you use.

What is the best angle for photos?

For most people, the camera at or slightly above eye level, with your face turned a little to one side rather than straight on, looks natural and flattering. Avoid shooting from far below, which distorts proportions.

How do I pose naturally?

Move a little instead of freezing: shift your weight, angle one shoulder toward the camera, keep your hands busy, and breathe out gently before the shot. A soft, genuine expression beats a forced smile.

Why do I look different in photos than in the mirror?

Mirrors show a flipped, familiar view in three dimensions, while photos are flat, unflipped, and affected by lens distance and lighting. Small changes in light, angle, and distance explain most of the difference — not a flaw in how you look.
